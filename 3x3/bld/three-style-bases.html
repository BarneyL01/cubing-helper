How it works

  1. A base is a commutator that solves one pair with no setup. For example [R' D' R , U2] moves buffer → A → P. Your Orozco bases are three of them: buffer → O → B, buffer → H → B and buffer → L → B.
  2. Its reverse (the two parts swapped) solves the pair the other way round: buffer → P → A. Like Orozco forwards and backwards, each group below shows both.
  3. Any other pair: find a base whose positions your targets can reach with D and L turns. The setup moves a target into its base position (for example D' moves K into the G position), the base does its cycle, and the setup is undone. The buffer never moves.

Each pair shows what its setup moves, for example "K into G". Open a pair for the steps, pictures and a cube to watch.

Which base? Read it from the letters

Every base belongs to one of four families, and the two letters tell you which. The D turn inside the insertion follows the same pattern in every family as Orozco's O, H, L: first letter D, second D', third D2.

  • Three letter sets, each in the order D, D', D2: O H L (your Orozco), G P S (like the satnav), L T H (the P family).
  • Who stays at home: in Orozco, B: R first, so the insertion swaps out the B position. In the G P S family, the buffer: R' first, so the insertion swaps out C's own position. In the P family both targets are on the bottom, so the middle turn is U and the interchange is a D turn.
  • The middle turn and the interchange are always on opposite layers: a D in the insertion goes with a U interchange, and a U with a D interchange.
