Laws of the cube

Three rules every position obeys, however it was scrambled. They explain why some things can never be done alone, and why algorithms that twist, flip or swap always do it to two pieces.

Our own explanation of standard cube theory. Every number on this page is measured by the simulator on the moves shown (js/cube-laws.js), and the laws were checked on thousands of random scrambles by log/2026-10-28-theory-check.mjs.

1. Corner twists add up to whole turns

A corner is twisted when its white or yellow sticker is not on the top or bottom. It can be a third of a turn clockwise or anticlockwise. Count the corners twisted clockwise and those twisted anticlockwise: the difference is always 0, 3 or 6. (The same thing said another way: all the twists add up to whole turns.)

  • So one corner can never be twisted on its own: two corners twisted opposite ways, or three the same way.
  • Your twist algorithm twists two corners, one each way.

2. Edge flips come in pairs

An edge is flipped when it is in a slot the wrong way round. The number of flipped edges is always even.

  • So one edge can never be flipped on its own: flip algorithms always flip two (or four).

An edge flip algorithm

3. Corner and edge swaps match

Count how many swaps of two pieces it would take to put every corner back in its place, ignoring twists. Do the same for edges. The two counts are both even or both odd, always.

  • Why: one quarter turn moves four corners round in a cycle (3 swaps) and four edges round (3 swaps). Each turn adds an odd number to both counts, so they stay matched.
  • So you can't swap just two corners (or just two edges): two corners swapped always comes with two edges swapped. Your Old Pochmann swap swaps two corners and the edges UL and UB.
  • A 3-cycle is two swaps (even), so commutators are always allowed on their own.

What one turn does

Measured on a solved cube after one move.

Slice moves (M, E, S) also move the centres, so on their own they seem to break law 3. M is the same as R and L' plus turning the whole cube, so measured against the centres, the laws still hold.
